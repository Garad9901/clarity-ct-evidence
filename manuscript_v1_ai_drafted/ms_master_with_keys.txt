@TITLE Explainable Artificial Intelligence for Chest CT: A Review of Recent Methods and a Proposed Concept-Based Reference Framework

@ABSTRACT
Deep learning models for chest computed tomography (CT) now reach high accuracy in screening and diagnosis, but clinicians, guideline groups and regulators increasingly ask how a model's output can be inspected. This narrative review, supported by a structured search, examines how explanations for chest CT models were produced and evaluated in work published in 2025 and 2026. Bibliographic sources and publisher sites were searched in October 2026; 146 records were verified and 84 sources were included, 72 of them from 2025 or 2026. The literature is organised into five families: post hoc attribution maps; concept-based models; anatomically structured, case-based and counterfactual methods; report-grounded vision-language explanations; and the evaluation of explanations. Among 27 chest CT studies charted, 15 presented the explanation only through examples or reported no assessment of it, seven reported a quantitative measure, three an expert rating or agreement measure, and two a reader study. Concept-based models for chest CT remain few, lobe-level structure is used for quantification but rarely for explanation, and report-generating models link text to organs or regions and not to individual lesions. Evidence on whether explanations help readers comes mainly from mammography, chest radiography and dermoscopy. On this basis the review proposes five desiderata for clinically usable explanations, a reference design (CLARITY-CT) that combines lobar pooling with a radiological concept layer, and a non-compensatory Clinically Meaningful eXplanation Index (CMXI) with stated formal properties. The design and the index are proposals that have not been implemented or tested, and a staged validation plan is outlined.

@KEYWORDS Explainable artificial intelligence; Chest computed tomography; Concept bottleneck models; Saliency maps; Vision-language models; Evaluation of explanations

# Introduction

Lung cancer was the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer death worldwide in 2022, with almost 2.5 million new cases and about 1.8 million deaths {Bray2024}. Low-dose computed tomography (CT) screening is now being delivered to large high-risk populations {Bhamani2025}, and projections based on insurance claims indicate that imaging use will continue to rise with population growth and ageing {Christensen2025}. Chest CT is therefore one of the settings in which deep learning is most likely to be used at scale.

Recent evaluations show both the promise and the fragility of such models. A nodule malignancy-risk model trained on one screening trial reached areas under the curve (AUC) between 0.94 and 0.98 when tested on 4,146 participants pooled from three European screening trials {Antonissen2025}, and dedicated artificial intelligence (AI) software has shown promise as a first reader for ruling out negative baseline screening scans {Walstra2025}. Performance does not always transfer, however. When an open-source lung cancer risk model was tested in 18,057 adults from an Asian health check-up centre, its AUC for predicting future cancers fell to 0.56 in never-smokers and light smokers {LeeJH2025}. A reader who cannot see why a model produced a given output has little basis for recognising when it has moved outside the conditions in which it works.

This is the practical case for explanation, and it is contested. One position holds that post hoc explanation of black-box models is unreliable in high-stakes settings and that models should be interpretable by design {Rudin2019}. A review of the published arguments concluded that fully autonomous black-box use is ruled out, but that a general requirement for explainability is not established where a clinician remains in the loop {Blackman2025}. Guidance has nevertheless moved towards explanation as something to be justified and tested. The FUTURE-AI consensus guideline, developed by 117 experts from 50 countries, lists explainability among its six principles and recommends that the need for explanations be decided with end users and that explanations be evaluated both computationally and with end users {Lekadir2025}. The European Society of Radiology notes that medical imaging AI falls in the high-risk class of the European AI Act, which requires trained human oversight, and that methods and standards for transparency and explainability are still under development {Kotter2025}. A recent special report adds that the interaction between radiologists and AI output, including automation bias and interface design, has received far less study than stand-alone model accuracy {KimSH2026}.

Explainable AI in medical imaging has been reviewed repeatedly, from general surveys {Ahmed2026;Saw2025;Durak2026} to reviews of inherently interpretable models {Hou2026;WangC2026}, of clinician-centred evaluation {UdDin2026}, of shortcut learning {Vasquez2025}, and of single domains such as echocardiography {Ishii2026}, cancer imaging {Fotopoulos2026} and lung cancer {Koutoulakis2026}. Table 1 compares these reviews. None of them is dedicated to chest CT. The closest in subject covers lung cancer across CT, chest radiography, histopathology and other modalities, and 19 of its included articles concern histopathology {Koutoulakis2026}; in the scoping review of cancer imaging, CT accounted for 37.5% of studies and lung for 23.5% {Fotopoulos2026}. Chest CT nevertheless has properties that matter for explanation: it is volumetric, the lungs have a lobar anatomy that radiologists already use to report location, thoracic imaging has a standard descriptive vocabulary {Bankier2024}, and scans paired with reports are now available in large public collections {Hamamci2026}.

This review therefore has three aims. The first is to describe how explanations for deep learning models on chest CT were produced in work published in 2025 and 2026. The second is to chart how those explanations were evaluated. The third is to propose, from the gaps identified, a set of desiderata, a reference design and an explanation-quality index that later experimental work can implement and test. The proposed design and index have not been implemented or validated, and they are presented as proposals throughout.

# Methodology

## Search Strategy and Sources

This is a narrative review supported by a structured search. It is not a systematic review, and no protocol was registered. Reporting was informed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews where its items apply {Tricco2018}. Searches were run on 9 and 10 October 2026. The sources were the Crossref bibliographic database, the arXiv preprint server (queried through the alphaXiv interface), general web search, and the article pages of publishers and proceedings series, including Springer Nature, BMC, the Radiological Society of North America, Frontiers, MDPI, BMJ, JMIR, the Proceedings of Machine Learning Research, and the proceedings of the NeurIPS, IJCAI and ICLR conferences. PubMed, PubMed Central, Europe PMC, ScienceDirect and IEEE Xplore could not be queried directly during the search. Articles in Elsevier and IEEE journals were therefore reached only through Crossref records, repository copies or preprint versions, and they are likely to be under-represented.

Seven themes were searched: earlier reviews of explainable AI in medical imaging; post hoc attribution on chest CT; concept-based models; anatomically structured, prototype-based, counterfactual and attention-based methods; vision-language and report-generating models for CT; the evaluation of explanations; and the clinical, regulatory and reporting context. Search terms combined the method names of each theme (for example gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM), Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), concept bottleneck, prototype, counterfactual, report generation, faithfulness and reader study) with terms for chest CT, lung and thoracic imaging.

## Eligibility and Selection

Records were eligible if they were published or first posted online between 1 January 2025 and 10 October 2026, were written in English, and either applied or evaluated an explanation method for a deep learning model on chest CT, or provided methodological, human-factors or regulatory evidence directly relevant to explaining such models. Peer-reviewed journal articles and papers from established conferences were preferred. Preprints were retained only where no peer-reviewed source covered the same point, and they are identified as preprints in the reference list. Because few studies have evaluated explanations on chest CT itself, studies from chest radiography, mammography, other radiological modalities, dermoscopy and ophthalmology were admitted for the evaluation theme, and their modality is stated wherever they are cited. Older sources were limited to original method papers, datasets and current standard documents.

Each candidate was opened on its publisher, proceedings or repository page, its bibliographic details were checked against that page or its Crossref record, and the page was checked for a retraction notice. In total 146 records were verified and charted, and 84 were included: 72 from 2025 or 2026 and 12 older sources (Fig. 1). The 62 records not included were older sources beyond those needed as originals or standards (n = 21), preprints without a peer-reviewed version that were not essential (n = 13), studies outside chest CT for which a closer source was retained (n = 12), records whose details could not be verified sufficiently or whose reporting was internally inconsistent (n = 11), and studies redundant with a retained study of the same task (n = 5). For 13 included sources only the abstract, or a preprint version of the full text, could be read; these are cited for their main conclusions only. Four included articles carry a published correction that could not be read, and no numerical result is quoted from them.

## Post Hoc Attribution Maps

Attribution methods produce a map of the input regions that most influenced an output of a trained model. Grad-CAM weights the feature maps of the last convolutional layer by the gradients of the class score, giving a coarse heat map that needs no change to the network {Selvaraju2020}. SHAP assigns each input feature an additive importance value for an individual prediction {Lundberg2017}. Both remain the default in chest CT. A class activation mapping method appeared in every one of the 12 attribution studies charted, in one of them only as a comparator, and in six it was used alongside SHAP, local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME), integrated gradients or attention maps (Table 3).

The clinical tasks were broad. They covered nodule malignancy on LIDC-IDRI {Muller2025}, resolution of screen-detected nodules in the NELSON trial {WangJ2025}, classification of lesions as benign or malignant {Hassan2026;Kodipyaka2026}, occult lymph node metastasis {Yan2026} and extranodal extension {WangJ2026} in lung cancer, COVID-19 {Rahat2026}, patterns of interstitial lung disease {ZhangJ2025}, preserved ratio impaired spirometry and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease {Mou2026}, pulmonary thromboembolism on CT pulmonary angiography {LiY2026}, nontuberculous mycobacterial disease {LiuCJ2025}, and classification of 18 abnormalities on volumetric scans {DiPiazza2026}. Seven of the 12 studies tested their model on data from another centre or another public dataset, so multi-centre validation of the prediction is now common.

Validation of the explanation has not kept pace. In nine of the 12 studies the maps were shown for a few examples or no assessment was stated: four cases in one study {WangJ2025}, two in another {LiuCJ2025}, two images {Rahat2026}, five patients {ZhangJ2025}, a single figure {DiPiazza2026}, overlays that were not compared with the annotated emboli {LiY2026}, visual inspection for attention to lung parenchyma {Mou2026}, example figures alongside feature-level SHAP plots {WangJ2026}, and no assessment mentioned in the abstract {Yan2026}. Three studies went further. One radiologist rated 150 maps pooled across chest CT and two magnetic resonance datasets and found that transformer attention maps marked the correct slice in 136 of them, against 37 for Grad-CAM applied to a three-dimensional convolutional network {Muller2025}. A variant of LIME with lung-region masking was tested with deletion, insertion and stability metrics on a public set of 1,097 CT slices, without a reader study {Hassan2026}. Two raters, whose expertise is not stated, judged the clinical relevance of Grad-CAM maps in six cases, with a Cohen kappa of 0.45 {Kodipyaka2026}. None of the 12 studies scored the overlap between a map and a lesion annotation.

## Concept-Based Models

A concept bottleneck model first predicts concepts that people have specified and labelled, and then predicts the target from those concepts alone, so that a user can correct a concept and see the output change {Koh2020}. The approach has a known weakness: when soft concept predictions are passed to the label predictor, information unrelated to the concepts can leak through, which lowers concept accuracy and makes interventions less effective {Havasi2022}. Recent surveys describe a rapid growth of such models, including concepts generated by large language models {WangC2026;Hou2026} and designs that relax the strict bottleneck {WangC2026}.

On chest CT the evidence is thin and concentrated on one task. Four studies were found, all on pulmonary nodule malignancy and all relying on radiologist-rated nodule attributes from LIDC-IDRI, a public collection of 1,018 CT cases annotated by four thoracic radiologists {Armato2011}. One fine-tuned a vision-language model so that nodule views align with sentences built from radiologist-annotated semantic features, and tested it on three external cohorts; prediction of the features themselves was acceptable for margin and pleural attachment but poor for most internal features {Zhuang2025}. A second tied each capsule of a capsule network to one attribute and built the malignancy output from attribute prototypes; in a study with six radiologists, the attribute scores and prototypes tended to raise diagnostic accuracy when the model was right and to lower it when the model was wrong {Gallee2025}. A third combined concept learning with an additive model that exposes the contribution of each attribute to the malignancy score {Dumaev2025}. A fourth, a preprint, distilled concept bottlenecks from frozen CT foundation models and reported modest concept fidelity, with external discrimination no better than nodule size alone {Tushar2026}. None of the four reports an analysis of concept leakage in the text that could be read, and no concept-based model for diffuse lung disease on CT was found.

Work outside chest CT indicates what such models can offer. An expert-guided concept bottleneck for chest radiographs gave concept explanations closer to radiologist reasoning than post hoc methods {Rafferty2025}. A model that matches concepts generated by a large language model to the layers of a biomedical vision-language encoder outperformed other concept-based models on seven datasets; its results tables report classification metrics only {WangC2025}. A concept-querying model that accounts for the uncertainty of each concept prediction was the most accurate interpretable-by-design approach on four of five datasets {Nahiduzzaman2026}. In ophthalmology, concept-based support raised the accuracy of six inexperienced doctors, while two senior doctors were essentially unchanged {WuY2025}, and a concept-enhanced biomedical foundation model was reported to help clinicians verify predictions {Nie2026}. For thoracic imaging, the Fleischner Society glossary supplies a current, consensus vocabulary from which a concept set could be drawn {Bankier2024}.

## Anatomically Structured, Case-Based and Counterfactual Methods

A second route to interpretability is to build structure into the model so that the output itself is the explanation {Rudin2019}. On chest CT the natural structure is the lobe. A lobe-based transformer on the COPDGene and ECLIPSE cohorts predicts the progression of emphysema separately for each lobe, so that its output is a lobe-by-lobe statement that can be compared with measured decline {Curiale2026}. A commercial tool that reports the volume of each interstitial pattern per lobe was compared with expert visual scoring in 107 patients. Agreement on overall extent was good for ground-glass opacity (kappa 0.70) and consolidation (0.60), moderate for reticulation (0.37) and poor for honeycombing (0.16), and agreement at the level of individual lobes was lower {Kuzmanovic2026}. Any lobe-level method depends on the lobe masks. In a benchmark on 164 scans, public lobe segmentation tools lost accuracy on complex anatomy; a locally trained model segmented more of the few cases with missing lobes, although the authors caution that these cases were too few to generalise {Amini2025}. No study was found that uses lobe masks to aggregate the evidence of a classifier lobe by lobe as an explanation.

Case-based reasoning explains an output by pointing to similar examples. The prototypical part network classifies an image by comparing its parts with learned prototypes {ChenC2019}; it has been applied to breast tomosynthesis with assessment through radiologist feedback {Berti2025}, but no peer-reviewed prototype network for chest CT was found other than the attribute prototypes described above {Gallee2025}. A reader study of a similar-case retrieval system for interstitial lung disease, with 28 cases and three readers, found that resident accuracy rose from 35.7% to 53.6% when retrieval was optional, a difference that was not statistically significant; overall time per case did not differ significantly {Bottcher2025}.

Counterfactual methods show what would have to change for the output to change. A preprint extends a latent-shift method to whole three-dimensional CT classifiers and reports that the mean pleural effusion score of 77 positive scans fell from 0.24 to 0.15 for their counterfactuals, with no clinical reader validation {Cohen2025}. On chest radiographs, counterfactual edits validated by radiologists exposed classifiers that relied on unrelated pathology as a shortcut {Cooke2026}.

Attention weights are often presented as explanations, with little support. In survival prediction for lung cancer, attention pooling over slices was not significantly better than average pooling, and the weights were inspected only qualitatively {Paolo2026}. For pulmonary embolism, a model trained with about a quarter of the slice labels matched full supervision (AUC 0.928 against 0.932), but its explanations were limited to example Grad-CAM maps {HuZ2025}. Uncertainty estimates are a complementary output. Conformal prediction on a head CT haemorrhage detector flagged 143 of 150 cases on which readers disagreed {Gamble2025}; conformal risk control on a nodule detector reached 91.4% sensitivity at 2.25 false positives per scan for nodules marked by at least three radiologists {Hulsman2025}; and uncertainty-driven training reduced the calibration error of nodule classifiers by up to 65% {Tripodi2026}. None of these studies pairs the uncertainty output with an explanation method.

## Report-Grounded and Vision-Language Explanations

Large collections of scans paired with reports have made text a possible form of explanation. CT-RATE provides 25,692 non-contrast chest CT scans from 21,304 patients with their reports and supports a contrastive model for zero-shot detection and a chat model {Hamamci2026}. These models return scores for the whole volume and free text that is not tied to a location. A CT vision-language model trained on abdominal scans with diagnosis codes and reports likewise offers generated text and organ segmentation, without grounding at the level of a finding {Blankemeier2026}.

Several designs bring the text closer to the image. Aligning each anatomical region with the report sentences that describe it ties a prediction to a named organ {Shui2025}. Region-guided report generation writes the findings region by region for 10 anatomical regions; the authors report that recognition of the region was high for eight of them but low for lung and pleura, and that the method works at organ level and can miss or mislocate individual lesions {ChenZ2025}. Generating one sentence for each predicted abnormality makes every sentence traceable to a named prediction, with no spatial localisation {DiPiazza2025}. A region-aware language model organises the report by anatomical region and was evaluated on the quality of the report, not on whether the regional grounding was faithful {Kyung2026}. In each case the unit of explanation is an organ or region, which is coarser than the lesion a radiologist would point to.

Two resources may change this. RadGenome-Chest CT adds to CT-RATE segmentation masks for 197 region categories, 665 thousand grounded reports and 1.2 million grounded question-answer pairs, built largely automatically, with the validation set checked manually {ZhangX2025}. A preprint describes 3,142 chest CT scans in which 8,028 report findings are linked to three-dimensional segmentations, with test sets annotated by radiologists {Baharoon2025}. The second makes it possible to test whether a model can point, voxel by voxel, to the evidence for a sentence.

Whether generated text is a faithful explanation is a separate question, and the available evidence comes from outside chest CT. In 478 emergency patients, with same-day CT as the reference, four of five models that generate chest radiograph reports produced hallucinated findings in 5.4% to 17.4% of reports {Lim2026}. A perturbation test of step-by-step rationales in six vision-language models found that answer accuracy and explanation quality diverged, and that mentioning a cue did not mean the reasoning was grounded in it {Moll2026}. In a randomised vignette experiment with 101 radiologists, a step-by-step explanation from a large language model raised diagnostic accuracy by 12.2 percentage points over no support {Spitzer2026}. No study of the faithfulness of text explanations for chest CT was found.

## How Explanations Are Evaluated

An explanation can be wrong in ways that are not visible. Randomisation tests showed that some saliency methods produce maps that barely change when the parameters of the model are randomised {Adebayo2018}, and on chest radiographs each of eight saliency methods failed at least one of four criteria of trustworthiness and localised abnormalities less well than dedicated networks {Arun2021}. Recent proposals separate the properties to be tested. Authors from the United States Food and Drug Administration set out four criteria for heat maps, namely consistency, plausibility, fidelity and usefulness, with a reporting card and a case study on synthetic mammograms {Lago2026}, and an educational review for radiologists distinguishes plausibility from faithfulness and stresses that explanations are tools for oversight, not proof of correctness {Durak2026}.

Three recent results show how fragile current measurements are. Many of ten explanation methods changed under perturbations that left the predicted class unchanged, and under natural corruptions none was best on every stability metric {Repetto2026}. In a preprint on breast magnetic resonance imaging that compared seven saliency methods, the ranking of methods by deletion score depended on the masking strategy {Poolpol2026}. Among seven brain magnetic resonance models with similar accuracy, only two based their decisions on plausible structure according to Grad-CAM {Jelitzki2026}. Shortcut learning has also been exposed: chest radiograph classifiers stayed well above chance with the lungs masked out {Sourget2026}, and an explanation method traced the errors of a COVID-19 detector to text-like marks on the images {Kauffmann2025}. A scoping review concluded that most medical imaging studies neither test for nor report such shortcuts {Vasquez2025}.

In practice, evaluation is uncommon. In radiological cancer imaging, 48% of 371 studies reported no validation of the explanation, and among those that did, 53.9% relied on expert or user judgement and 5.2% on quantitative metrics {Fotopoulos2026}. In echocardiography, explanations were evaluated only qualitatively in 40.4% of end-to-end model instances against 1.6% of instances in multistep pipelines with an interpretable intermediate representation {Ishii2026}. A review of lung cancer studies found that only six articles compared explanation methods with each other {Koutoulakis2026}, and a general systematic review named the lack of standard interpretability measures as a gap {Ahmed2026}. The chest CT studies charted here follow the same pattern: 15 of 27 presented examples only or reported no assessment, seven reported a quantitative measure, three an expert rating or agreement measure, and two a reader study (Table 3, Fig. 3).

@FIGURE 3 | How the explanation was assessed in the 27 chest CT studies charted, by family of explanation. Numbers inside the bars are counts of studies. The expert rating or agreement group includes one study in which the expertise of the raters was not stated

Studies with readers show that explanations can help and can mislead. A systematic review of 51 studies in which clinicians evaluated explanations found that example-based and concept-based explanations were associated with gains, whereas attribution maps mainly improved perception, and it judged the evidence methodologically fragile {UdDin2026}. In mammography, heat maps reduced automation bias from 36.1% to 17.8% of deliberately manipulated readings among six radiologists {Pesapane2026}; yet region prompts from a commercial tool were associated with a fall in reader sensitivity from 71% to 39% for cancers that the tool had missed {Taib2026}. Among 76 dermatologists, balanced accuracy rose from 79.9% with a plain prediction to 82.7% with an explainable system {Chanda2025}. A narrative review for radiologists concluded that current explanation methods are not yet mature enough for clinical use because their outputs are hard for medical experts to interpret {Saw2025}. No reader study of saliency explanations on chest CT published in 2025 or 2026 was found.

# Proposed Reference Framework

The gaps described above suggest what an explanation for a chest CT model should provide and how its quality might be summarised. This section sets out five desiderata, a reference design and an index. All three are proposals. They have not been implemented, and no performance is claimed for them.

## Desiderata for Clinically Usable Explanations

Table 4 lists five requirements, each stated so that it can be measured. Faithfulness and stability correspond to the fidelity and consistency criteria proposed for heat maps {Lago2026}. Anatomical grounding is intended to follow the lobar description of location that is conventional in chest imaging. Semantic expressibility asks that the explanation can be stated in standard terminology {Bankier2024}. Actionability asks that the explanation supports a counterfactual question. On the evidence reviewed, attribution maps address faithfulness only in part and the other requirements hardly at all, and concept-based models address semantic expressibility but, without spatial supervision, not anatomical grounding.

@TABLE 4 | Proposed desiderata for a clinically usable explanation of a chest CT model
Code | Requirement | Proposed measurement | Component of CMXI
E1 | Faithfulness: removing the evidence that the explanation identifies should lower the output more than removing other evidence | Normalised gap between insertion and deletion curves | F
E2 | Anatomical grounding: evidence is reported by lung lobe | Share of positive attribution that falls in the lobes annotated as containing the finding | P
E3 | Semantic expressibility: the explanation can be stated in standard radiological terms | Accuracy of predicted concepts against radiologist annotation | Assessed separately
E4 | Stability: inputs that cannot be told apart give explanations that cannot be told apart | Local sensitivity of the attribution to small perturbations of the input | S
E5 | Actionability: the explanation answers what would have to change for the output to change | Counterfactual necessity and sufficiency of the identified evidence | Enters F
@TABLEFOOT CMXI Clinically Meaningful eXplanation Index. Conciseness (component Q of the index) is an additional property and not a separate desideratum

## CLARITY-CT Reference Design

CLARITY-CT is the name given here to a design in which the representation is divided by lobe before any prediction is made and every prediction passes through named radiological concepts (Fig. 4). A lobe segmentation model supplies masks $A_\ell$ for the five lobes, $\ell = 1,\dots,L$, and their union $A^{+}$; the reliability of these masks limits everything that follows {Amini2025}. A volumetric encoder produces a feature vector $\phi_u$ at each position $u$ of a coarse grid. Features are pooled within each lobe with learned attention weights $\alpha_u^{(\ell)}$ that sum to one over the lobe, and the lobar descriptors are combined in proportion to lobe volume:

@EQ 1 | z_\ell=\sum_{u\in A_\ell}\alpha_u^{(\ell)}\phi_u,\qquad z=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\pi_\ell\left(z_\ell+e_\ell\right),\qquad \pi_\ell=\frac{|A_\ell|}{|A^{+}|}

where $e_\ell$ is a learned lobe embedding. Concepts are predicted for each lobe and for the whole lung with shared weights, so that a concept has the same meaning wherever it is evaluated, and each finding $c$ is predicted from the concepts through non-negative weights:

@EQ 2 | c_{k\ell}=\sigma\left(w_k^{\top}z_\ell+b_k\right),\qquad \hat{y}_c=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{ck}^{+}c_k+\gamma\,v_c^{\top}r+b_c\right),\qquad W_{ck}^{+}\ge 0

Here $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $c_k$ is the whole-lung value of concept $k$, and $r$ is a residual pathway with gate $\gamma$ that carries information not expressible in the concepts. Non-negative weights mean that raising a concept can only raise the probability of a finding that it supports. Because a residual pathway could let information bypass the concepts, which is the leakage problem described for concept bottleneck models {Havasi2022}, the design reports a leakage gap, defined as the loss of discrimination when the residual is switched off:

@EQ 3 | \Delta_{\mathrm{leak}}=\mathrm{AUC}\left(\hat{y}\right)-\mathrm{AUC}\left(\hat{y}\mid\gamma=0\right)

An initial vocabulary of 23 descriptors based on Fleischner Society terminology {Bankier2024} is listed in Online Resource 1; it is a starting point to be revised with thoracic radiologists. The uncertainty of each concept prediction can be carried forward, as has been done for concept-querying models {Nahiduzzaman2026}.

Anatomical grounding is encouraged during training. Let $a_u^{(c)}$ be the attribution of finding $c$ at voxel $u$ and $M_c$ the set of lobes recorded as containing the finding. The anatomical evidence mass of lobe $\ell$ and the alignment penalty are

@EQ 4 | \mathrm{AEM}_\ell(c)=\frac{\sum_{u\in A_\ell}\left[a_u^{(c)}\right]_{+}}{\sum_{u\in A^{+}}\left[a_u^{(c)}\right]_{+}+\epsilon},\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}=\frac{1}{|C^{+}|}\sum_{c\in C^{+}}\left(1-\sum_{\ell\in M_c}\mathrm{AEM}_\ell(c)\right)

where $[x]_{+}=\max(x,0)$, $\epsilon$ is a small constant and $C^{+}$ is the set of findings present. The penalty needs only a record of which lobes contain each finding, which is weaker supervision than voxel-level masks. It constrains where evidence may come from and not its shape, and because the denominator runs over the lungs it does not by itself penalise evidence placed outside them.

Lobar pooling has one property that can be stated exactly. For any differentiable head $F$ applied to $z$, the first-order contribution of lobe $\ell$ is

@EQ 5 | \Delta_\ell F=\pi_\ell\left\langle\nabla_z F,\;z_\ell+e_\ell\right\rangle,\qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Delta_\ell F=\left\langle\nabla_z F,\;z\right\rangle

so the lobar contributions add up exactly to the gradient-times-input attribution of the head, with no interpolation between a coarse map and the image. If $F$ is linear in $z$, as the concept layer is up to its link function, the decomposition is exact and not only first-order. The statement and its short proof are given in Online Resource 1. It concerns the assignment of evidence among lobes; the mapping from voxels to features within a lobe remains approximate. It follows from the linearity of the pooled descriptor, would hold for any partition of the features, and says nothing about whether the evidence is clinically correct. Alongside the lobar and concept outputs, the design returns similar past cases through prototypes of the lobar descriptors {ChenC2019} and a prediction set from conformal calibration {Gamble2025;Hulsman2025}.

@FIGURE 4 | CLARITY-CT reference design. The representation is divided by lobe before prediction, and each finding is predicted through named radiological concepts. The design has not been implemented

## CMXI Explanation-Quality Index

The Clinically Meaningful eXplanation Index (CMXI) summarises four measurements of an explanation, each scaled to the interval from 0 to 1. Let $\mathrm{ins}(q)$ be the output retained when only the top fraction $q$ of attributed voxels is kept and $\mathrm{del}(q)$ the output retained when that fraction is removed, both divided by the original output and limited to the unit interval. Faithfulness $F$, plausibility $P$, stability $S$ and conciseness $Q$ are defined as

@EQ 6 | F=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathrm{ins}(q)-\mathrm{del}(q)\right)dq\right],\qquad P=\sum_{\ell\in M_c}\mathrm{AEM}_\ell(c)

@EQ 7 | S=\exp\left(-\kappa\max_{\|\delta\|_2\le\varepsilon\|x\|_2}\frac{\|a(x+\delta)-a(x)\|_2}{\|\delta\|_2}\right),\qquad Q=\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N}i\,\tilde{a}_{(i)}}{N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{a}_{(i)}}-\frac{N+1}{N}

where $\kappa>0$ and $\varepsilon>0$ are constants fixed in advance, and $\tilde{a}_{(1)}\le\dots\le\tilde{a}_{(N)}$ are the sorted absolute attributions, so that $Q$ is their Gini coefficient. Removal of voxels is proposed to use replacement by healthy tissue from the same lobe and not zeroing. Whatever strategy is used must be declared, because rankings of methods by deletion score depend on it {Poolpol2026}. The index is the weighted geometric mean

@EQ 8 | \mathrm{CMXI}=F^{w_1}P^{w_2}S^{w_3}Q^{w_4},\qquad w_i>0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{4}w_i=1

The geometric form is chosen for four properties, proved in Online Resource 1. The index is zero whenever any component is zero. It increases strictly with each component. It is non-compensatory, in that the gain in one component needed to offset a small loss in another grows without bound as the second approaches zero. It scales in proportion when all components are scaled together. A weighted arithmetic mean has neither the first nor the third property: it gives a positive score to an explanation with zero faithfulness, and it allows a map concentrated on a single voxel to offset near-zero faithfulness through high conciseness (Fig. 5).

@FIGURE 5 | Behaviour of the geometric aggregate used in CMXI compared with an arithmetic mean as faithfulness approaches zero, with the other three components held at 0.8 and equal weights. The curves are computed from the definitions and are not experimental results

The index has clear limits. The weights are not fixed here; they should be set by structured elicitation with thoracic radiologists before the index is used, and equal weights are the neutral default. Plausibility requires lobe-level annotation of findings. Stability is defined as a maximum over perturbations and can only be estimated by sampling, so the estimator and its error have to be reported. The insertion and deletion curves are undefined when the original output is zero, so faithfulness applies only to findings with a non-zero output, and the attribution method and the fractions at which the curves are sampled must be declared. Conciseness rewards concentrated attributions whether or not they are correct, and it is computed on absolute attributions whereas plausibility uses only positive ones; both choices remain open. The index covers desiderata E1, E2 and E4 and adds conciseness, while E3 and E5 are assessed alongside it (Table 4). It measures computational properties only. In the terms of the four criteria proposed for heat maps it does not measure usefulness {Lago2026}, and guidance asks that explanations be evaluated with end users as well as computationally {Lekadir2025}. Whether a higher CMXI corresponds to explanations that help readers is an empirical question that this review cannot answer.

# Discussion

Four observations follow from the work reviewed. First, post hoc heat maps remain the default explanation for chest CT models, and in most studies they are shown and not tested. This agrees with counts from cancer imaging {Fotopoulos2026} and echocardiography {Ishii2026}, and it persists even in studies whose predictions were validated across centres. Second, the alternatives that reviews of other modalities describe are barely represented on chest CT. Concept-based models were found only for nodule malignancy, and lobe-level structure is used to quantify disease but was not found in use to explain a classifier. Third, report-generating models have moved from text about the whole scan towards text tied to an organ or region, but not yet to the lesion, and their grounding has been evaluated less than their wording. Fourth, almost all evidence on whether explanations change what readers do comes from other modalities.

That evidence argues against treating any explanation as harmless. Attribute explanations tended to lower accuracy when the model was wrong {Gallee2025}, region prompts were associated with missed cancers when the tool missed them {Taib2026}, and heat maps reduced but did not remove automation bias {Pesapane2026}. Evaluations with readers therefore need to include cases on which the model is wrong, and they need to record reliance and reading time as well as accuracy. Guidance already asks for this dual evaluation {Lekadir2025}, and regulation in Europe requires human oversight while its standards for transparency are still being written {Kotter2025}. The argument that explanation is not always required when a clinician stays in the loop {Blackman2025} does not remove the need to know what an explanation does to that clinician.

Chest CT is well placed for progress on these points. The lobes give a reporting frame that is already clinical. A consensus vocabulary exists {Bankier2024}. Public data now pair scans with reports {Hamamci2026}, with region-level grounding {ZhangX2025}, and, in a preprint, with lesion-level segmentations of reported findings {Baharoon2025}. The framework proposed here is one way to combine these resources. Its contribution is conceptual: it states requirements that can be measured, a design in which evidence is assigned to lobes exactly at the level at which it is reported, and an index that cannot be raised by one property at the expense of another.

## Limitations

This review has several limitations. The search was structured but not systematic, was run over two days, and could not query PubMed, ScienceDirect or IEEE Xplore directly, so relevant studies will have been missed and statements that no study was found are provisional. Searching and charting were assisted by a generative AI tool and checked by the authors, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was made. The window of 2025 and 2026 excludes earlier work on chest CT explanation, which is represented only through original method papers. Thirteen sources were read as abstracts or preprint versions. The evidence map in Table 3 covers the studies selected and is not a census of the field. Evidence on reader effects was drawn from other modalities and may not transfer to volumetric CT. No radiologist took part in this review or in the design of the proposal, so the clinical statements, the proposed desiderata and the initial concept vocabulary have not been checked by a thoracic radiologist. Finally, the proposed design and index are untested. No claim is made about their accuracy, their computational cost or their value to readers, and the formal properties of the index do not establish that it measures what matters clinically.

## Directions for Validation

A staged plan would test the proposal. The first stage is implementation on public data: CT-RATE for multi-abnormality prediction with reports {Hamamci2026}, LIDC-IDRI for nodule attributes {Armato2011}, and the grounded datasets for region-level and lesion-level evidence {ZhangX2025;Baharoon2025}, with the lobe masks checked on difficult anatomy before use {Amini2025}. The second stage is computational evaluation under a fixed protocol: the four components of CMXI for the proposed design and for standard attribution baselines, randomisation checks {Adebayo2018}, a declared masking strategy {Poolpol2026}, concept accuracy against radiologist labels, and the leakage gap. The third stage is external testing, because performance on one population does not guarantee performance on another {LeeJH2025}. The fourth stage, which requires ethics approval and thoracic radiologists, is a reader study that includes cases on which the model is wrong and measures accuracy, reliance and reading time, with the weights of the index elicited beforehand. Reporting should follow established reporting standards for AI studies, as consensus guidance asks {Lekadir2025}.

# Summary

In the work from 2025 and 2026 charted for this review, explanations for chest CT models were dominated by post hoc heat maps that were rarely tested: 15 of the 27 chest CT studies charted presented examples only or reported no assessment of the explanation. Among the studies found, concept-based models for chest CT were confined to nodule malignancy, lobe-level structure was used for quantification but not for explaining classifiers, and report-generating models grounded their text in organs or regions and not in lesions. Evidence that explanations help or harm readers comes from other modalities and points in both directions. This review proposes five measurable desiderata, a reference design that divides the representation by lobe and predicts through radiological concepts, and a non-compensatory index of explanation quality with stated formal properties. These proposals are untested. Their value depends on the implementation, external testing and reader studies outlined here.
